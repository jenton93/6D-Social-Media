# How do you like to be told there's a problem?

It's a question we ask, and it surprises people that we ask it.

Because something will go wrong. Not might — will. Kit breaks, people make
mistakes, shows change. Anyone who's done enough of these knows the plan is a
starting position, not a promise.

What varies isn't whether it happens. It's what you want to hear when it does.

## Four clients, four completely different right answers

**Some want every detail.** They want to know the projector failed, which one,
what we swapped it for, and how long the room was without it. Telling them
less feels like being managed, and being managed makes them nervous.

**Some just want to hear it's in hand.** Give them the same detail and you've
handed them something to worry about that they can't do anything with. They're
not being incurious — they've got a hundred other things on and they hired you
so this wouldn't be one of them.

**Some like seeing the workings.** The head-scratching, the discussion in the
corner, the second option being tried. It reassures them. A show that appears
effortless makes them wonder what they're paying for.

**And some never want to believe there was a problem at all.** They want to
walk out thinking it went exactly to plan — and if you tell them otherwise
afterwards, you've taken something away from them for no reason.

Same event. Same fault. Four different jobs.

## Getting it wrong is its own kind of failure

This is the part that's easy to miss, because it doesn't show up in a debrief.

You can deliver a technically flawless show and still leave the client uneasy
— because you told them nothing for three hours and they spent those three
hours assuming the worst. Or because you kept walking over with updates they
didn't want and couldn't act on.

Nobody writes that down as a fault. It just quietly affects whether they book
you again.

## Which is why we ask

Usually early, usually in the first proper conversation, and usually more
bluntly than people expect: *when something goes sideways on the day, how do
you want to hear about it, and from whom?*

⛔ *If there's a version of this you actually say out loud, use yours — it'll
be better than mine.*

It also tells us who needs to be in that conversation. Sometimes the person
running the event wants everything and the person sponsoring it wants nothing.
That's worth knowing at the start rather than working out at four in the
afternoon with a broken screen.

## On a repeat show, nobody has to ask

Which is the practical argument for keeping the same people on a client's
events.

The crew already know that this client wants the detail and that one doesn't.
They know which of the two people in the room to walk over to. They know the
CEO doesn't want a countdown timer and needs one anyway.

None of that is written down anywhere. It sits with the people who were there
last time.
