Design direction

Six photographs, used large. This is where each one goes, why the department grid deliberately has none, and which pictures we can publish without asking anyone's permission.

About the images on this page. They're generated abstract blocks, not stock and not real shows, marked Placeholder in the corner. They're here so the layout can be judged with something in the frame rather than empty boxes. Abstract on purpose: stock carries a licence, and it would make this mockup look better than the site can actually launch, which is the wrong thing for a design decision to rest on.

Allocation

Six photographs, and where each one goes

With six, the decision that matters is not which pictures to take but which ones to leave out. Two rules hold the whole thing together: no image appears small, and no page carries two. Break either and six starts to look like a shortage.

Why the departments get none

Six small photographs of six dark rooms all look like the same show. They would consume the entire library to say less than the words already say, and they would put every image below the size where it does any work.

The grid is stronger typographic anyway. A producer reading it is scanning for whether we cover their gap, and a list answers that faster than a picture does.

Why the workshop earns a slot

It's the only shot that isn't of an event, and it's doing a different job from the other five. The others prove we deliver shows. The workshop proves there is a business behind them: premises, tooling, stock, people who were there on a Tuesday.

For the verify job, that photograph is worth more than a fourth show.

Where they get reused. Shot 03 appears on both the home page and the Work page, and shot 06 on both About and Set & Staging. That's fine: nobody reads two pages of a supplier site in one sitting closely enough to notice, and it's much better than padding with a weaker seventh image.
Why photo-led

Photographs are the proof we can publish without asking permission

The urgent job is verify. Someone has our name and searches it. Copy can be written by anyone in an afternoon; a photograph of a real build, in a real venue, with real crew in it, cannot be faked or borrowed. It does that job in under a second, before anyone reads a sentence.

That matters more here than most places, because the two things that would normally do this work aren't available to us. Testimonials are awkward when the work is white-label, since asking an agency to praise its subcontractor asks them to admit they used one. Named case studies need written permission per client, which is still outstanding.

There's a second reason, and it's about register. The voice is deliberately quiet. Quiet copy on a plain page reads as thin. Quiet copy with strong photography reads as confident, because the pictures carry the scale so the words don't have to. It's the combination that lets us stop making claims.

Which is why the one rule that cannot bend is no stock. Every competitor uses it and everyone in this industry recognises it on sight. It says either that we have no work of our own to show, or that we think the reader won't notice. Both are worse than an empty page.

Clearance

Which photographs we can actually publish

The client-naming rule is usually read as a rule about text. It isn't. A wide shot of a branded set identifies the client as clearly as writing their name, so every photograph sorts into one of three tiers first.

Tier 1 · use freely

Nothing in frame says whose show it is

Crew on a build. Truss, motors, bridles. Distro and cable runs. The workshop. A dark room before doors with the screens blank. Detail: a desk under worklight, a connector panel.

This tier is the workhorse, and it happens to be the most on-brand material we have. Black rooms, cool light, people working. Nobody else bothers to shoot it because it isn't glamorous, which is exactly why it reads as real.

Tier 2 · quick check

Identifiable, but nothing branded

A full stage with screens lit but showing no readable content. A room of people from behind. Recognisable venue architecture.

Usually fine. One line to the client, "happy for us to use this?", and it takes a minute. Do it before publishing rather than after.

Tier 3 · written permission

A reader could say whose show it was

Logos, branded content on screen, a set carrying an identity.

Same process as naming a client, and worth doing for two or three, because these are the shots that prove scale most convincingly.

For whoever is holding the camera: a lot of Tier 3 becomes Tier 1 by moving three feet or shooting a moment earlier. The same stage, framed before content goes up, or shot from the side to lose the screen, is clean. Get in the habit of taking both.
How photographs are used

Four rules, and the reasons

Do

  • Full bleed, edge to edge. No gutter, no rounded corners, no shadow, no border. It reads as confident and it makes a small library look bigger, because each image gets more room
  • Black as the ground. The logo is a black circle and the work happens in dark rooms. Dark sections let the photographs be the light source: they glow, the page recedes
  • Text next to pictures, or on the flat black between them. The one exception is the hero, where the image is chosen for the space rather than the space forced onto the image
  • Cyan as a signal. Eyebrows, rules, one word in a headline, links. It appears rarely enough to mean something

Don't

  • Headlines over busy images. This is where most supplier sites fall apart, and the usual fix is a black gradient over the whole picture, which ruins the photograph to save the text
  • A white page with photo cards. Every image becomes a bright rectangle in a bright field, and the whole thing reads as a brochure about venues rather than the inside of one
  • Cyan as a background field, or as a tint over a photograph. A lot of what we shoot already has cool blue-white light in it, so the accent belongs to the pictures rather than being applied on top
  • Middle type sizes. Big headings and normal body, nothing in between. Middle sizes are what make a page look like a template

Getting them

Six is a morning's work, not a project

Most of this is a phone in the right hand at the right moment. Modern phones are fine in dark rooms, and a slightly grainy real photo beats a polished stock one every time.

On the next show

Shots 01, 02 and 03

Three moments, ten minutes each. Mid-build with crew in frame, for 02. After the room is lit, before doors, from the back or a high side, for 01. From the audience position once it's running, for 03.

From the archive

Shots 04 and 05

Two more shows, deliberately different from each other and from 03. A conference, an awards night, and something with a different shape.

If the archive has nothing usable, these are the two to plan for on the next two jobs rather than the two to compromise on.

Worth doing on top, and it's free. The same room from one fixed spot, three times in a day: empty, mid-build, finished. Three photographs, thirty seconds each, from a position you're standing in anyway. Nobody in this industry publishes it and it explains the entire business without a word of copy. If it comes off, it replaces shots 01 and 02 and becomes the best thing on the site.
The way this fails

Six is enough, and it's also the floor

Six large photographs make a better site than twenty small ones, so this isn't a compromise. But it only works if all six are good, because there is nowhere for a weak one to hide. At this count every image is above the fold of something.

Being blunt about the failure mode: the copy has been waiting since January. If the design now waits on a photo library, the site doesn't launch this year either. So build the slots so that swapping or adding a photograph later is an upload, not a layout change, and launch on whatever is ready.

If only four exist on the day, drop shots 04 and 05 and run one case study instead of three. Do not pad the gap with a weaker image, and do not pad it with stock.
