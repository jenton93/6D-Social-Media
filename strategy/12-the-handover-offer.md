# The Handover Offer

> *"When a client moves from the QEII, we want to offer support for free
> pre-event — making sure their new supplier isn't ripping them off, but also
> quoting the job, showing what they had before. I feel this will help build
> the relationship, which is far more valuable than any single event."*

The instinct is right and the idea is strong. It uses the one thing we have
that's genuinely hard to copy — knowing what a show actually needs — and
spends it on someone at the exact moment they're most exposed.

It also directly addresses the demand we already know exists: clients leave
the building, don't get the same service, and come back asking. This meets
them *before* the disappointment rather than after it.

Three things need settling first. The second one matters most.

---

## 1. Doing both at once undermines the first

Offering to check a competitor's quote *while also quoting the job* isn't
neutral advice, and a sophisticated client will see that instantly. Done
carelessly it reads as a sales tactic wearing a favour's clothes — which costs
more trust than the favour builds.

The fix isn't to hide the interest. **It's to declare it, immediately and
without being asked.**

> *"Happy to look over what you've been sent. You should know we'd quote it
> too if you wanted — so read my comments with that in mind, and if you'd
> rather I just looked at theirs and stayed out of it, that's genuinely fine."*

Saying that out loud makes the advice *more* credible, not less. It's also the
kind of thing people repeat to colleagues afterwards.

**And then mean it.** If a client says "just review it", review it and don't
bid. One of those is worth more over five years than a single job — which is
exactly the argument being made here.

## 2. ⚠️ Talk to QEII Live first

This is the one to sort before offering it to anybody.

QEII Live is a client and one of the strongest credentials we have. A client
of theirs leaves the building, and we follow them out and quote them directly.
However fair that is, it can look like disintermediation — and if they hear
about it from their client rather than from us, it looks worse.

The defence is genuinely good, so make it in advance rather than afterwards:

- The event is at another venue. QEII Live **can't** service it — there's no
  work being taken from them
- The client keeps having a good experience with the people they associate
  with the QEII, which reflects well on the building
- We'd rather they knew and were comfortable than found out sideways

There's an even better version available: **do it jointly.** Offer it as
something QEII Live extends to their departing clients, with us delivering it.
That makes them look good, keeps us inside the relationship, and removes the
awkwardness entirely. ⛔ *Worth exploring — costs one conversation.*

Same principle applies to any agency or in-house team whose client we'd
follow. Ask first.

## 3. "Ripping them off" can never be the external framing

Internally it's a fair description of what we're watching for. Externally it
breaks the rule that holds everywhere else: **never brief against other
suppliers.** A client who hears "we'll check they're not fleecing you" learns
that we talk about competitors that way — and wonders what we say about them.

Reframe it as helping them buy well:

| Don't say | Say |
|---|---|
| "Make sure they're not ripping you off" | "Make sure you're comparing like with like" |
| "They'll pad the quote" | "We'll tell you what's missing from the scope" |
| "You're being overcharged" | "Here's what this normally involves, so you can ask better questions" |
| "We'd have done it cheaper" | "Here's what you had last time, so nothing gets forgotten" |

The last one is the strongest thing in the whole offer and it costs nothing:
**a client moving venue has no record of what they had.** They don't know the
screen sizes, the crew count, the comms spec or what was in the rider. We do.
Handing that over is pure value with no comparison involved.

---

## What the offer should actually be

Name it plainly. ⛔ *Something like:* **"Moving venue? Take your spec with
you."**

Three things, all cheap, none requiring anyone to share a competitor's price:

1. **A written record of what they had last time.** Kit, crew, timings, what
   worked and what didn't. This is ours to give and nobody else has it
2. **A read of the new brief or scope** — what's missing, what to ask about,
   what usually goes wrong in a room like that
3. **A sanity check on the schedule** — get-in windows, rehearsal, turnaround
   (`202609-blog-03`)

If they want us to quote as well, we quote. If they don't, we don't, and we
still do 1–3.

**Cap it.** ⛔ *An hour or two per client, and only for clients we've actually
delivered for.* Free work consumes the scarcest resource in the business, and
an uncapped offer becomes a job nobody scheduled.

---

## Why this is worth doing even when it wins nothing

Two returns beyond the immediate one:

- **It makes leaving reversible.** A client who has a bad experience elsewhere
  and remembers we helped anyway comes back. One who heard nothing from us
  doesn't
- **It's a referral engine.** "They helped us even though we weren't using
  them" is a story people tell. In a market this small, told stories are how
  every job has arrived for ten years

## As content

This is Job 1 and Job 3 at once, and it's genuinely publishable — nobody else
offers it.

- **A page or section:** *"Moving to a new venue?"* — the offer, plainly
  stated
- **A post:** the value of having a written record of your own event, and how
  few clients do
- **A director post:** why we help clients who aren't currently paying us.
  Slight risk of sounding self-congratulatory — the version that works is
  practical and unsentimental, about how this market actually operates rather
  than about us being generous

would be a poor way for them to find out.*
